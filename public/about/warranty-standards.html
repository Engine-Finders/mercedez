Warranty Types — What's the Difference?

The warranty you receive depends on the type of engine you choose. Each has different coverage and terms.

🔄 Used Engine

Warranty: Typically 3–6 months on supply only (sometimes labour too). Check carefully.

Coverage: Usually covers catastrophic failure only — not wear items like injectors or pumps.

Our view: This is the minimum you should accept. We recommend at least 6 months on used units.

🔧 Reconditioned Engine

Warranty: Typically 12–24 months on parts and labour.

Coverage: Comprehensive coverage for the reconditioned components — usually excludes ancillaries that were reused.

Our view: This is the most common standard in our network and represents good value.

🏗️ Rebuilt Engine

Warranty: Typically 24–36 months on parts and labour, often with additional terms.

Coverage: Most comprehensive — often includes a full refresh of all major components.

Our view: Best peace of mind, but costs more upfront. Suitable for long‑term keepers.

We recommend always asking for a warranty that covers both supply and fit, regardless of engine type. Our cost comparison guide explains the trade‑offs in more detail.

What to Check Before You Sign the Warranty

Don't just accept a warranty — read it. These are the things we recommend every Mercedes owner verifies.

  1. Is it written and signed? Verbal assurances are worthless. It must be a physical or digital document with the specialist's signature.
  2. Does it cover both parts and labour? Some warranties only cover the engine unit — not the labour to remove and refit it if something goes wrong. That can cost more than the unit itself.
  3. What is the duration? 12 months is our minimum standard. If it's less, ask why. If it's significantly more, check the terms for catch‑clauses.
  4. Are there mileage caps? Unlimited mileage is our standard. If a specialist caps mileage at 10,000 miles per year, that's a red flag — it suggests they don't trust their own work.
  5. What are the exclusions? Every warranty has some exclusions (e.g., damage from overheating caused by a faulty cooling system). Make sure they are specific and reasonable.
  6. Who do you contact to claim? The document should give a clear name, phone number, email address, and process for making a claim.
  7. Does it transfer to a new owner? If you plan to sell the vehicle, check that the warranty transfers. This adds value to the car and is a sign of a quality specialist.

If a specialist refuses to provide a written warranty meeting these standards, we recommend not booking with them and comparing alternative quotes instead.

Warranty Red Flags to Avoid

We see these warning signs in sub‑standard warranties. Walk away if you spot them.

  • ❌ "We provide a warranty" — but nothing in writing. If they won't put it in writing, it doesn't exist.
  • ❌ Warranty duration varies based on "how much you spend". This suggests the specialist is price‑gouging rather than standing behind their work.
  • ❌ Labour not covered. A warranty that only covers the part is next to useless — the labour to replace a failed unit can be £800–£1,500.
  • ❌ Unreasonable exclusions. For example, excluding coverage if you drive the car "too hard" — what does that mean? It should be defined.
  • ❌ No claims contact details. If the document doesn't say who to call or email, you'll be chasing when something goes wrong.
  • ❌ Warranty starts from the date of purchase, not fitting. If you buy a unit and store it for six months, that's six months less coverage. The clock should start when it's fitted.

If a specialist has any of these red flags, we recommend checking their vetting status and comparing with other specialists in our network.

How to Make a Warranty Claim

If something goes wrong, you need a clear route to get it fixed — without arguments, delays, or unexpected bills.

  1. Contact the specialist immediately — use the contact details in the warranty document. Don't delay; many warranties have a "report within 48 hours" clause.
  2. Describe the problem clearly — what happened, when, and any warning signs you noticed. If possible, take photos or videos.
  3. Return the vehicle to the specialist for inspection. They are entitled to inspect the issue before authorising any work.
  4. Wait for diagnosis — the specialist will determine if the failure is covered. If they refuse, ask for a written explanation and check it against the warranty terms.
  5. If the claim is approved, the specialist will carry out the repair or replacement under warranty. You should not be charged for covered work.
  6. If the claim is refused and you believe it should be covered, escalate in writing and involve us. We can review the case and, if appropriate, log it in our corrections log and review the specialist's status in our network.

Our vetting process includes assessment of a specialist's complaint‑handling and claims history, so we know before we approve them that they are likely to honour warranties.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the minimum warranty you require from specialists?

At least 12 months on both parts and labour, with unlimited mileage, clear claims process, and written documentation before work begins.

Do all specialists in your network meet these standards?

Yes — it's a mandatory part of our specialist vetting process. We review warranty documents before approving a specialist and monitor complaints afterwards.

What's the difference between supply-only and supply-and-fit warranty?

Supply-only covers the engine unit itself. Supply-and-fit covers both the unit and the labour to install it — so if the engine fails and needs replacing, the labour is also covered.

Can I extend the warranty if I want longer coverage?

Some specialists offer extended warranty options for an additional fee. Check the terms carefully — extended warranties often have stricter conditions.

What if the specialist refuses to honour the warranty?

Contact us immediately. We will review the case, and if the specialist is at fault, we will remove them from our network and log the outcome in our corrections log.

Does the warranty cover consequential damage?

Most warranties do not cover consequential damage (e.g., if the engine failure damages the car's electronics or bodywork). We recommend checking your insurance policy for that type of coverage.
