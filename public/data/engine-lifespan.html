Methodology

How we measure lifespan, what the data includes, and its limitations.

Source: Internal CRM quote-request data from EnginesMarket.co.uk and EngineFinders.co.uk, combined. Full calendar year 2025. 24,650 unique quote requests across all brands; 5,920 relate specifically to Mercedes-Benz. Lifespan estimates are derived from mileage-at-enquiry data, supplier notes on typical failure mileage, and cross-referenced with UK MOT history for a subset of vehicles where full mileage records were available.

What we mean by "lifespan": the mileage at which an engine is first reported as needing a major repair or replacement (excluding routine servicing items like belts, filters, or consumables). This is not a mean time to failure in the engineering sense – it is the observed point at which owners contact us for a replacement, which is the most practical measure for decision-making.

Limitations: our dataset over-represents engines that have already failed (by definition). To avoid survivorship bias, we pair enquiry data with MOT mileage histories for a control sample of vehicles that have not been reported to us. This gives a more balanced view of typical lifespan across the whole parc, not just the failures.

Refresh cycle: annually, each February. This is our first edition and establishes the baseline year.

Factors That Affect Engine Lifespan

Not all engines of the same type last the same distance – these are the variables that make the biggest difference.

Service History

Engines with full Mercedes-spec service history (oil changes every 10,000 miles or annually) consistently outlast those with patchy records by 30,000–40,000 miles. The OM651 in particular is highly sensitive to oil quality and extended intervals [PATTERN DATA].

Driving Patterns

Diesels used predominantly on motorway/A-road runs see significantly longer lifespans than those used for short urban trips – the DPF, EGR and AdBlue systems last much longer when they can regenerate properly. The OM642 swirl flap failure is far more common on city-driven examples [EM-INTERVIEW].

Model / Chassis

The same engine fitted to a heavier vehicle (e.g. GLE vs C-Class) tends to fail earlier – the V6 diesel in a 2.5-ton SUV works harder than in a saloon. Our data shows OM642 in GLE/ML chassis averaging 130,000–150,000 miles vs 150,000–170,000 in E-Class [PATTERN DATA].

Preventative Maintenance

Owners who proactively replace timing chains, water pumps and EGR coolers at 80,000–100,000 miles regularly push engines beyond 180,000 miles. This is especially true for the M272 (balance shaft) and OM651 (timing chain) – a £1,000 preventative job can save a £4,500 replacement [EM-INTERVIEW].

Which Mercedes Engines Last Longest?

The top three for outright longevity in our 2025 dataset.

OM654

160,000+ miles – the modern 2.0-litre modular diesel. Well-documented examples reaching 200,000 miles with regular servicing. AdBlue/EGR issues are the main weak point, but they are repair‑able rather than fatal [PATTERN DATA].

2

M276

150,000+ miles – the 3.5-litre naturally aspirated V6. One of Mercedes’ most durable petrol engines. Timing chain stretch is the main issue, but it usually appears gradually and is fixable before catastrophic failure [EM-INTERVIEW].

3

OM642

140,000–170,000 miles – the 3.0-litre V6 diesel. Very capable of high mileage, but swirl flap and oil cooler seal repairs are expensive. Well-maintained motorway‑use examples regularly exceed 180,000 [PATTERN DATA].

All lifespan figures are observed from our 2025 dataset and MOT control sample – individual results vary. A full service history is the single most important predictor of high mileage.

What This Means For Owners

If you're buying a used Mercedes, the lifespan table above gives you a realistic expectation for each engine family. An OM651 with 120,000 miles is not "over the hill" – it's entering the zone where preventative maintenance matters most. A £500 timing chain tensioner check at 100,000 miles can add another 40,000 miles of life [EM-INTERVIEW].

For owners facing a major repair, the repair-to-value calculation remains our recommended decision framework. The 70% threshold (specific to Mercedes and BMW in our data) means a replacement is financially sound if the cost is less than 70% of the vehicle's current market value. An OM651 replacement at £3,500 on a £6,000 W204 C‑Class (58%) is a clear "go" – the same job on a £3,500 car (100%) is a "scrap". Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a Mercedes engine last on average?

Based on our 2025 data, the average Mercedes engine reaches 120,000–180,000 miles before a major repair is needed. The OM654 and M276 are at the upper end (150,000+), while older OM646 and M272 units are at the lower end (90,000–130,000).

Which Mercedes engine lasts the longest?

The OM654 2.0-litre modular diesel and the M276 3.5-litre V6 petrol consistently top our lifespan charts, with well-maintained examples regularly exceeding 150,000 miles – and some OM654s reaching 200,000.

Does the OM651 have a limited lifespan?

No – the OM651 is a 120,000–150,000-mile engine on average. It's the most-enquired engine because there are so many of them, not because it's unusually short-lived. Preventative timing chain tensioner checks at 80,000 miles significantly improve its longevity.

How can I make my Mercedes engine last longer?

Stick to Mercedes-spec oil and change it every 10,000 miles or annually (whichever comes first). For diesels, avoid repeated short trips – give the DPF a chance to regenerate with a 20-minute motorway run once a week. For petrol V6/V8s, change the coolant every 3 years and listen for timing chain rattle at startup.

Is 120,000 miles a lot for a Mercedes diesel?

No – 120,000 miles is comfortably within the expected lifespan of every diesel engine in our table. It's the mileage where preventative maintenance becomes critical, not the mileage where failure is imminent. Many OM642 and OM651 examples go on to 180,000 with proper care.

Does high mileage mean I should scrap the car?

Not automatically – use the repair-to-value rule. If the cost of the repair is less than 70% of the car's market value (Mercedes threshold), replacement is financially sensible. High mileage itself is not a reason to scrap; a worn engine can be replaced, but a rotten body or obsolete electronics may be the real write-off trigger.
