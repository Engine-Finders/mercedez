Mileage-at-Failure Distribution

When do M178 engines typically need replacement or major repair?

60k–80k

Early failures — typically coolant system issues or turbocharger seal problems. These are relatively rare but occur on hard-driven, poorly maintained examples [PATTERN DATA].

80k–120k

Peak failure window — most M178 enquiries fall in this range. Oil leaks and coolant system work dominate. Proactive maintenance is the differentiator [PATTERN DATA].

120k–160k

Extended-life examples — well-maintained M178s regularly exceed 140,000 miles. These have immaculate service histories with proactive coolant and oil system work [PATTERN DATA].

The M178's mileage-at-failure distribution reflects its use in the AMG GT range — many are weekend or track cars, so mileage accumulates slowly. The peak failure window of 80,000–120,000 miles aligns with what specialists report as the point where the M178's oil and coolant systems need attention [EM-INTERVIEW]. Unlike the older M157 hot-vee design, the M178 has better heat management, but it's still a high-performance twin-turbo V8 — heat is the enemy.

Most Common Failure Modes (Ranked)

What actually fails on the M178, in order of frequency observed in our data.

1. Oil Leaks — Valve Covers

The most common M178 failure — oil leaks from the valve covers, often appearing at 60k–100k miles. The hot-vee design creates heat that degrades gaskets over time. Repair requires significant disassembly but is well-documented by AMG specialists [PATTERN DATA].

2. Coolant System Leaks

Turbocharger coolant lines and the main cooling system are prone to leaks, particularly on older examples. The M178's improved heat management reduces this vs the M157, but it's still a known issue. Early detection prevents turbocharger damage [PATTERN DATA].

3. Turbocharger Seal Issues

Less common than on the M157 but still a known issue — turbocharger seals can degrade, leading to oil consumption or in severe cases, turbo failure. Reconditioning turbos is the standard remedy; new units are extremely expensive [PATTERN DATA].

4. Auxiliary Battery Drain

M178-powered AMG GTs are known for battery drain issues, linked to the auxiliary battery system. While not a direct engine failure, it's a common complaint that often leads owners to investigate more serious issues — and sometimes precipitates a major engine inspection [PATTERN DATA].

How Long Does an M178 Actually Last?

The data-driven answer on M178 lifespan.

Based on our 2025 enquiry data and cross-referenced with workshop feedback [EM-INTERVIEW], the M178 typically reaches 120,000–160,000 miles before a major repair is required. This is an excellent lifespan for a high-performance twin-turbo V8 — better than the older M157 (110k–150k) and comparable to the naturally aspirated M156 (100k–130k) but with more complex turbos.

The M178's improved heat management is the key difference from the M157. Mercedes learned from the M157's hot-vee issues and redesigned the cooling system, moved the turbos to a more accessible position, and improved oil flow. The result is an engine that's more durable and, when it does fail, more repairable than its predecessor [EM-INTERVIEW].

What separates a 120,000-mile M178 from a 160,000-mile one is almost always maintenance history. Engines with annual coolant system inspections, proactive oil leak repairs at the first sign, and strict adherence to oil change intervals (5,000–7,000 miles) consistently outlast those where the first sign of trouble was a warning light. The AMG GT's high value means owners are often willing to invest in preventative maintenance — and the data shows it pays off.

Our Verdict — The M178 Decision

Is an M178 failure a reason to scrap the car? The data says no.

The M178 is a ✅ Safe engine for repair — here's why.

With host vehicle values ranging from £30,000 to £120,000 [THIRD-PARTY] and a reconditioned supply cost of £8,000–£14,000 [EM-QUOTE], the replacement cost typically sits well below the 70% threshold that would trigger a scrap decision. Even at the upper end of the replacement cost (£14,000 + £1,430 labour for an AMG specialist), the total fitted cost of around £15,400 is still only 13–50% of the vehicle's value on a typical AMG GT.

But there's a caveat: the M178 is a specialist engine. Not every workshop can work on it — you need an AMG specialist with the right tools and training. Fortunately, the UK has a strong network of AMG specialists, many of whom are part of our vetted network. The high value of AMG GT vehicles means the investment in a proper specialist is almost always justified.

For owners facing an M178 failure, the key is early diagnosis and choosing the right specialist. A valve cover oil leak caught at 60,000 miles is a relatively straightforward repair. The same leak ignored until 100,000 miles can lead to turbocharger damage, oil starvation, and a much larger bill. Regular inspections and addressing any leaks immediately are the two single most effective actions an owner can take.

Frequently Asked Questions

How reliable is the Mercedes M178 engine?

The M178 is a very robust high-performance V8 when properly maintained. It typically reaches 120,000–160,000 miles before needing major repair — an excellent lifespan for a twin-turbo engine. The M178 benefits from improved heat management over its predecessor (the M157), making it more durable and less prone to catastrophic failures.

What are the common signs of M178 failure?

Oil leaks from the valve covers (visible as oil on the engine), coolant loss with no visible external leak (coolant system issues), oil consumption between services (turbo seals), and battery drain (auxiliary battery system). A cold-start rattle is less common than on older AMG V8s but can indicate timing chain tensioner wear.

How much does an M178 replacement cost?

A reconditioned M178 supply costs £8,000–£14,000 [EM-QUOTE]. Labour for removal and replacement is 16–22 hours at AMG-specialist rates (£65/hr indie, £100+ at main dealer), adding £1,040–£1,430 for independent specialists. Total fitted cost ranges from £9,040 to £15,430. Main dealer costs would be significantly higher.

What's the difference between M177 and M178?

Both are 4.0-litre twin-turbo V8s based on the same architecture. The M177 is used in the E63, C63, S63 and GLC63 — it's a "front-mid" installation. The M178 is the dry-sump version used specifically in the AMG GT range (GT, GT S, GT C, GT R), where the engine is mounted lower and further back for better weight distribution. They share most internal components but the M178 has different cooling, oiling and ancillaries.

Should I buy a high-mileage M178?

Yes, but with caveats. A high-mileage M178 (80,000+ miles) is a viable proposition if it has a full service history showing regular oil changes (every 5,000–7,000 miles) and coolant system work. Look for evidence of valve cover gasket replacement — if it hasn't been done, budget for it. The host vehicle value at 80,000 miles is typically £40,000–£70,000, and a well-maintained M178 can easily reach 150,000 miles.

Is the M178 worth maintaining long-term?

Absolutely — the AMG GT range holds its value well, and the M178 is a robust, well-supported engine. The cost of major maintenance (valve covers, coolant system) is significant but still far less than depreciation on a newer car. For an AMG GT owner, investing in engine maintenance is a sound financial decision.
