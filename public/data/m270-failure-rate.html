Most Common Failure Modes (Ranked)

What actually fails on the M270, in order of frequency observed in our data.

1. Water Pump Failure

The most common M270 failure — water pump leaks or complete failure, typically appearing at 60k–100k miles. The plastic impeller can fail, leading to overheating. Replacement is straightforward and relatively inexpensive, but ignoring it can cause head gasket failure [PATTERN DATA].

2. Timing Chain Tensioner Wear

The M270's timing chain tensioner can wear, particularly on earlier examples (pre-2015). A cold-start rattle is the early warning sign. If caught early, the tensioner alone can be replaced; if ignored, the chain can jump, causing catastrophic damage [PATTERN DATA].

3. Carbon Buildup (Intake Valves)

Direct-injection engines like the M270 suffer from carbon buildup on intake valves, leading to rough idling, misfires, and reduced power. Walnut blasting is the standard remedy, typically needed at 60k–100k miles. Regular use of premium fuel and occasional Italian tune-ups can help reduce buildup [PATTERN DATA].

4. Thermostat Housing Failure

The thermostat housing can crack or fail, leading to coolant leaks and temperature instability. Usually replaced alongside the water pump as a preventative measure. Relatively inexpensive and well-documented repair [PATTERN DATA].

How Long Does an M270 Actually Last?

The data-driven answer on M270 lifespan.

Based on our 2025 enquiry data and cross-referenced with workshop feedback [EM-INTERVIEW], the M270 typically reaches 130,000–160,000 miles before a major repair is required. This is an excellent lifespan for a modern turbocharged petrol engine — comparable to the later M274 and better than many competitors in the premium compact segment.

The M270's longevity is a testament to its robust design. It's a relatively simple turbo-four with a cast-iron block and aluminium head, and when properly maintained, it's capable of high mileage. The key failure points — water pump, timing chain tensioner, carbon buildup — are all predictable and repairable [EM-INTERVIEW].

What separates a 130,000-mile M270 from a 160,000-mile one is almost always maintenance history. Engines with annual coolant system inspections, proactive water pump replacement at 60,000–80,000 miles, and regular oil changes consistently outlast those where the first sign of trouble was a warning light. The M270's relatively low replacement cost (£1,500–£3,000) means that even when a major failure occurs, it's often financially viable to repair rather than scrap the car.

Our Verdict — The M270 Decision

Is an M270 failure a reason to scrap the car? The data says no.

The M270 is a ✅ Safe engine for repair — here's why.

With host vehicle values ranging from £6,000 to £22,000 [THIRD-PARTY] and a reconditioned supply cost of £1,500–£3,000 [EM-QUOTE], the replacement cost typically sits well below the 70% threshold that would trigger a scrap decision. Even at the upper end of the replacement cost (£3,000 + £715 labour for an independent specialist), the total fitted cost of around £3,700 is still only 17–60% of the vehicle's value on a typical M270-powered A-Class, B-Class, CLA or GLA.

But there's a caveat: the M270 is fitted to a wide range of vehicles with very different values. A £3,700 repair on a £6,000 A-Class W176 (62% of value) is borderline — if the car has other issues (rust, suspension wear, high mileage), scrapping might be the better option. On a £15,000 CLA or GLA, it's a clear "go". Use our Repair vs Replace Calculator to run the numbers for your specific vehicle.

For owners facing an M270 failure, the key is early diagnosis. A water pump leak caught at 60,000 miles is a £500–£1,000 repair. The same leak ignored until 100,000 miles can lead to head gasket failure and a much larger bill. Regular inspections and addressing any coolant loss immediately are the two single most effective actions an owner can take.

Frequently Asked Questions

How reliable is the Mercedes M270 engine?

The M270 is a very reliable modern turbocharged petrol engine. It typically reaches 130,000–160,000 miles before needing major repair — an excellent lifespan for a turbo-four. Key failure points (water pump, timing chain tensioner, carbon buildup) are well-understood and repairable, making it one of the more dependable Mercedes engines of its era.

What are the common signs of M270 failure?

Coolant loss with no visible external leak (water pump failure), cold-start rattle (timing chain tensioner wear), rough idling or misfires (carbon buildup), and overheating (thermostat or water pump). Regular coolant checks and listening for startup noises are the best early detection methods.

How much does an M270 replacement cost?

A reconditioned M270 supply costs £1,500–£3,000 [EM-QUOTE]. Labour for removal and replacement is 8–11 hours at independent specialist rates (£65/hr), adding £520–£715. Total fitted cost ranges from £2,020 to £3,715. Main dealer costs would be significantly higher.

What's the difference between M270 and M274?

Both are 2.0-litre turbocharged four-cylinder petrol engines. The M270 is the earlier version, used in W176 A-Class, W246 B-Class, C117 CLA and X156 GLA from 2012–2018. The M274 is the later, updated version with different engine management, revised timing chain components, and some internal improvements. The M274 is generally more refined and slightly more reliable, but both share the same basic architecture.

Should I buy a high-mileage M270?

Absolutely — the M270 is a robust engine and high-mileage examples (80,000+ miles) are viable if they have a full service history. Look for evidence of water pump replacement (around 60,000–80,000 miles) — if it hasn't been done, budget for it. Check for cold-start rattle, which suggests timing chain tensioner wear. A well-maintained M270 can easily reach 150,000 miles.

Is the M270 better than the M274?

They're very similar, but the M274 is generally considered a slight improvement — better engine management, revised chain tensioner design, and improved refinement. However, the M270 is not significantly less reliable; the main difference is age and availability of parts. Both are good engines, and the choice between them is usually dictated by the vehicle rather than the engine itself.
