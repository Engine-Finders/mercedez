Mileage-at-Failure Distribution

When do M272 engines typically need replacement or major repair?

40k–60k

Early failures — often balance shaft sprocket wear on specific engine numbers. Relatively uncommon but well-documented on pre-2008 M272s with affected serial numbers [PATTERN DATA].

60k–100k

Peak failure window — most M272 enquiries fall in this range. Balance shaft sprocket wear is the dominant issue. The check engine light often illuminates with timing-related codes [PATTERN DATA].

100k–150k

Extended-life examples — M272s that have had balance shaft work done can exceed 150,000 miles. These examples are well-maintained with proactive chain and guide replacement [PATTERN DATA].

The M272's mileage-at-failure distribution is dominated by the balance shaft sprocket issue — a known design flaw where the sprocket wears prematurely on certain engine serial numbers. This typically manifests at 60,000–100,000 miles with a check engine light and timing chain rattle. Engines with serial numbers up to a certain range are affected; later production (post-2008) has the revised, hardened sprocket [EM-INTERVIEW].

Most Common Failure Modes (Ranked)

What actually fails on the M272, in order of frequency observed in our data.

1. Balance Shaft Sprocket Wear

The M272's infamous failure — the balance shaft sprocket wears prematurely, causing timing chain slack and eventually timing chain jump. Early warning signs include check engine light (P0016/P0017 codes) and a rattling noise on cold start. Affects specific engine serial numbers. If ignored, catastrophic engine failure follows [PATTERN DATA].

2. Timing Chain Tensioner Wear

Often secondary to balance shaft sprocket wear, the timing chain tensioner can fail, increasing chain slack. A cold-start rattle lasting more than 1–2 seconds is the classic symptom. Replacing the tensioner alone is sometimes possible if caught early, but the sprocket usually needs attention too [PATTERN DATA].

3. Intake Manifold Flap Failure

The M272's variable intake manifold has flaps that can fail, causing rough running and reduced power. Often accompanied by check engine light codes. Replacement manifold is the standard fix, though some specialists offer flap repair kits [PATTERN DATA].

4. Oil Leaks — Camshaft Solenoids

The camshaft adjuster solenoid seals can leak oil, leading to oil consumption and potential camshaft adjuster issues. Relatively inexpensive to fix if caught early, but can contribute to timing chain problems if ignored [PATTERN DATA].

How Long Does an M272 Actually Last?

The data-driven answer on M272 lifespan.

Based on our 2025 enquiry data and cross-referenced with workshop feedback [EM-INTERVIEW], the M272 typically reaches 100,000–130,000 miles before a major repair is required. This is a shorter lifespan than the later M276 (150,000+) and significantly shorter than the diesel engines, but it's a function of the balance shaft design flaw rather than general fragility.

The key variable is whether the balance shaft sprocket has been replaced. M272s with the original affected sprocket typically fail at 60,000–100,000 miles. Those that have had the balance shaft replaced with the revised part can easily exceed 150,000 miles. The engine serial number is the critical factor — certain numbers are affected, others are not. We provide guidance on this in the FAQ below.

What separates a 100,000-mile M272 from a 130,000-mile one is almost always maintenance history and whether the balance shaft issue was addressed proactively. Listen for cold-start rattle at every startup — if the rattle lasts more than 1–2 seconds, get it inspected immediately. A £500 tensioner repair can prevent a £4,500 engine replacement.

Our Verdict — The M272 Decision

Is an M272 failure a reason to scrap the car? Often, yes — but it depends.

The M272 is a ⚠️ Watch engine — here's why.

With host vehicle values ranging from £3,000 to £12,000 [THIRD-PARTY] and a reconditioned supply cost of £2,500–£4,500 [EM-QUOTE], the M272 sits in a difficult position. A total fitted cost of £3,500–£5,500 on a £6,000 W204 C-Class (58–92% of value) means the repair can approach or exceed the 70% threshold that would trigger a scrap decision.

The tipping point: the M272's host vehicles are ageing. A W204 C-Class, W212 E-Class, CLS or SLK from 2005–2011 is now worth £3,000–£8,000 in average condition. A £4,500 engine replacement is 56–150% of the vehicle's value. On a £3,000 car, it's a scrap decision. On a £10,000 CLS or low-mileage E-Class, it's worth considering.

The one exception: if the balance shaft sprocket is caught early (before it causes timing chain damage), the repair can be limited to the sprocket and timing components — typically £1,500–£2,500 rather than a full engine replacement. Early detection is everything with the M272.

Frequently Asked Questions

How reliable is the Mercedes M272 engine?

The M272 is a generally good engine with one significant flaw — the balance shaft sprocket wears prematurely on certain engine serial numbers. This typically fails at 60,000–100,000 miles and can cause catastrophic engine failure if ignored. Engines with the revised sprocket (post-2008) or those that have had the repair done are reliable and can exceed 150,000 miles.

What are the common signs of M272 failure?

Check engine light with timing-related codes (P0016/P0017), a rattling noise on cold start lasting more than 1–2 seconds, rough idling, and reduced power. The rattle is the most important early warning sign — if you hear it, get the timing system inspected immediately.

How much does an M272 replacement cost?

A reconditioned M272 supply costs £2,500–£4,500 [EM-QUOTE]. Labour for removal and replacement is 12–16 hours at independent specialist rates (£65/hr), adding £780–£1,040. Total fitted cost ranges from £3,280 to £5,540. Main dealer costs would be significantly higher. The balance shaft sprocket repair, if caught early, is cheaper at £1,500–£2,500.

Which M272 engine numbers are affected by the balance shaft issue?

The balance shaft sprocket issue affects specific M272 engine serial numbers. Generally, engines with serial numbers below a certain threshold are affected. Pre-2008 M272s are the most susceptible. Always check the engine serial number before buying — if it's in the affected range, factor the repair cost into your purchase decision or use it as a negotiation point.

Should I buy a high-mileage M272?

Yes, but with significant caveats. A high-mileage M272 (80,000+ miles) is only viable if the balance shaft sprocket has already been replaced with the revised part. If it hasn't been done, the repair is coming — and it's expensive. Check the service history for evidence of timing chain or balance shaft work. If there's none, budget £2,000–£3,000 for the work or consider a different car.

What's the difference between M272 and M276?

The M272 is the older 3.5-litre V6 with the balance shaft issue, used from 2004–2011. The M276 is the later 3.5-litre V6 (and 3.0-litre turbo) that replaced it, with a much more robust timing chain design and no balance shaft sprocket issue. The M276 is significantly more reliable and has a longer lifespan (150,000+ miles). The M276 also has direct injection and produces more power. It's the engine to choose if you're looking for a reliable V6 Mercedes.
