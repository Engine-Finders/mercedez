Where the 306DT Sits in the Family

Land Rover's diesel V6 lineage runs in a fairly straight line, and knowing where the 306DT sits in it explains a lot about why it behaves the way it does.

The 276DT, a 2.7-litre V6, was the first of this family and appeared in the Discovery 3 and early Range Rover Sport L320. It carried its own well-documented weak point — oil pump housing failure — and was replaced from around 2009 by the larger, more powerful 306DT. The step from 2.7 to 3.0 litres brought more torque and a stronger bottom end in most respects, but the crankshaft bearing design that defines the 306DT's own failure mode was carried through largely unchanged. From roughly 2020–2021, Land Rover began replacing the 306DT with the AJ20D6, a genuinely different straight-six architecture rather than an evolution of the V6 — the two are sometimes confused because they share the same D300 badge on some models, which is covered in detail on our AJ20D6 fitment guide. Understanding this lineage matters practically: a 306DT and an AJ20D6 badged identically as "D300" are not interchangeable, do not share the same failure profile, and should never be diagnosed or quoted as if they were the same unit.

Known Failures

One catastrophic, well-documented weak point, and a small number of secondary issues that are far less urgent.

🔴

Crankshaft Bearing Failure

The defining risk. Catastrophic once advanced, typically 80,000–140,000 miles. Cold-start knock is the reliable early warning sign — full diagnosis and cost breakdown on the dedicated failure page.

🟡

Turbo Contamination

Usually a secondary consequence of advanced bearing wear rather than an independent failure — bearing material entering the oil circuit damages the turbo bearings downstream.

🟡

DPF Blockage

As with most modern diesels, urban-heavy driving accelerates DPF-related issues on the 306DT specifically because its regeneration cycle needs sustained higher revs to complete fully.

Buying Advice

What actually matters if you're looking at a 306DT-powered vehicle right now.

📋

Ask for the Full Service History

Not just stamps — actual invoices showing oil grade and interval. Extended intervals correlate strongly with earlier bearing wear in our data.

👂

Listen Cold

Insist on starting the vehicle from genuinely cold, ideally first thing in the morning before viewing. A warmed-up demonstrator hides the earliest warning sign.

💷

Price In the Risk, Don't Walk Away

An otherwise excellent Discovery 4 with patchy history is still often a good buy if the price reflects the genuine possibility of a future engine bill.

Common Misconceptions

  • "SDV6 fixed the TDV6's problems." It didn't — the badge change reflected a power and marketing update, not a bearing redesign. Both names carry the same underlying risk.
  • "A knocking diesel is normal." Some diesel clatter is normal; a knock that's new, worsening, or louder cold than warm is not, and dismissing it as "just a diesel" is the single most common reason owners miss the early window for cheaper intervention.
  • "If it hasn't failed by X miles, it's now safe." There's no hard cutoff. Risk reduces with documented history and trouble-free miles, but it doesn't reach zero.

Our Verdict

"A strong, torquey diesel let down by one well-documented weak point — manage that one risk and the 306DT is a genuinely dependable engine."

The 306DT is not a bad engine. It is a good engine with one serious, well-understood failure mode concentrated in a specific mileage band. On the vehicles it's typically fitted to, replacement is financially sound in the large majority of cases against our 60% threshold, and full service history with disciplined oil-change intervals materially reduces the risk of ever facing that decision at all. Owners who understand this specific risk and buy or maintain accordingly tend to get many trouble-free years out of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the 306DT a reliable engine?

Broadly yes, with one well-known catastrophic weak point: crankshaft bearing failure, typically between 80,000 and 140,000 miles. Outside of that specific failure mode, the 306DT is a capable, torquey V6 diesel with a strong reputation among independent specialists, and most examples with documented service history never experience the failure at all.

What vehicles have the 306DT engine?

Discovery 4, Range Rover L405, Range Rover Sport L320 and L494, and early Range Rover Velar L560 D300 models built before 2021. It was sold under both the TDV6 and SDV6 names depending on model year.

Is TDV6 the same engine as SDV6?

Yes. TDV6 and SDV6 describe the same 3.0 V6 diesel family, badged differently at different points in its production run — SDV6 replaced TDV6 as the marketing name from around 2013 without a fundamental change to the core engine architecture.

How long does a 306DT actually last?

With documented oil-change history at or ahead of interval, well past 150,000-180,000 miles is common. The crankshaft bearing risk is concentrated in a specific mileage band rather than being a certainty, and vehicles that pass through that window without symptoms rarely develop the fault afterward.

Should I buy a car with a 306DT if I can't see full service history?

Treat it as a real risk factor rather than a dealbreaker. Budget mentally for the possibility of a reconditioned engine, have a specialist listen for cold-start knock before you buy, and use that uncertainty to negotiate price rather than walking away from an otherwise good vehicle.
