M112 Camshaft Adjuster Failure - Symptoms, Cost & Diagnosis

Camshaft adjuster failure on the Mercedes M112 V6 engine is a serious issue that can cause timing chain problems, engine noise, and catastrophic engine damage if ignored. This guide covers everything you need to know.

Camshaft adjuster failure on the M112 V6 engine is a known weakness that can lead to timing chain issues, rough running, and potential engine destruction. Early diagnosis is critical to avoid a full engine rebuild.

Symptom Progression - What Owners Notice First

Camshaft adjuster failure on the M112 engine rarely happens without warning. The symptoms develop over time, and catching them early can save your engine.

  1. Stage 1 – Rough idle. The engine runs unevenly at idle, with occasional misfires. The check engine light may flash briefly.
  2. Stage 2 – Timing chain rattle. A rattling noise from the front of the engine, especially on cold start, indicates timing chain slack caused by a failing adjuster.
  3. Stage 3 – Loss of power. The engine feels sluggish, particularly at low RPM. Acceleration is poor, and the car may hesitate.
  4. Stage 4 – Check Engine Light. Fault codes P0011, P0012, P0021, or P0022 (camshaft position timing) appear. The engine may enter limp mode.
  5. Stage 5 – Timing chain jump. If the adjuster fails completely, the timing chain can skip teeth, causing valves to hit pistons. This leads to catastrophic engine damage.

If you notice any of these signs, do not drive the car. Have it inspected immediately. For a broader look at timing chain issues, see our M272 balance shaft chain guide.

Root Cause - Why M112 Camshaft Adjusters Fail

The M112 V6 engine uses a camshaft adjuster (also called a camshaft phaser) on each cylinder bank to vary valve timing for improved performance and efficiency. The adjuster is hydraulically operated using engine oil pressure. Over time, the internal locking pin or the spool valve can wear, causing the adjuster to fail to lock in position. This results in timing chain slack and erratic camshaft timing. The most common failure point is the internal locking pin, which can become stuck or break, allowing the camshaft to move out of sync with the crankshaft. Oil contamination from neglected oil changes can accelerate wear, as can using the wrong oil viscosity. The M112 engine is particularly susceptible to this issue, especially in higher-mileage vehicles [PATTERN DATA]. If left unaddressed, the timing chain can jump, causing valves to contact pistons.

What Makes Camshaft Adjuster Failure More or Less Likely

🛢️

Neglected Oil Changes

The adjuster relies on clean, high‑quality oil. Extended intervals or the wrong oil cause sludge buildup that blocks the hydraulic passages, leading to adjuster failure.

🔧

Incorrect Oil Viscosity

Using oil that's too thick or too thin can prevent the adjuster from functioning correctly. Always use Mercedes‑approved oil with the correct viscosity.

⚠️

Age and Mileage

Adjusters typically fail after 80,000–120,000 miles. The internal components wear out over time, especially in vehicles with poor maintenance histories.

⏱️

Short Trips

Frequent short journeys prevent the engine from reaching operating temperature, causing condensation and acid buildup that can damage the adjuster.

How a Specialist Confirms It

1

Diagnostic Scan (STAR)

Using Mercedes STAR diagnostic tool, the specialist reads fault codes related to camshaft timing (P0011, P0012, P0021, P0022). These indicate incorrect camshaft position.

2

Oil Pressure Test

Low oil pressure can prevent the adjuster from working correctly. The specialist checks oil pressure to rule out pump or oil level issues.

3

Timing Chain Inspection

The timing chain and adjuster are inspected by removing the valve cover. A worn adjuster may have visible play or broken internal components.

4

Camshaft Position Monitoring

Using diagnostic software, the specialist monitors camshaft position data while the engine is running. Deviations from expected values confirm adjuster failure.

Prevention & Early Detection

You can reduce the risk of camshaft adjuster failure and catch issues early.

  • Stick to strict oil change intervals. Use only Mercedes‑approved oil (MB 229.5) and change it every 10,000 miles or annually. Clean oil is critical for the hydraulic adjuster.
  • Use the correct oil viscosity. For the M112, 5W‑40 or 0W‑40 is typically recommended. Check your owner's manual.
  • Listen for timing chain rattle. A brief rattle on cold start is a warning sign – have it inspected before it causes damage.
  • Avoid short trips. Longer drives allow the engine to reach operating temperature, reducing sludge formation.
  • Address check engine lights promptly. If the camshaft position codes appear, don't delay – it could save your engine.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of camshaft adjuster failure on an M112 engine?

The earliest signs are a rough idle, a rattling noise from the front of the engine on cold start, and a check engine light with camshaft position codes.

Can I drive with a faulty camshaft adjuster?

No. If the adjuster fails completely, the timing chain can jump, causing valves to hit pistons. This leads to catastrophic engine damage. Stop driving and have it inspected immediately.

How much does it cost to replace an M112 camshaft adjuster?

At an independent specialist, expect £1,200–£1,800 for one adjuster, and £2,000–£3,500 if both adjusters and the timing chain need replacement. Main dealer prices are significantly higher.

Is it worth replacing the camshaft adjuster on an older M112?

It depends on the vehicle's value. On a car worth over £3,500, the repair is usually worthwhile. On a sub‑£2,500 car, evaluate whether the car is otherwise in good condition.

Can a camshaft adjuster failure be prevented?

Regular oil changes with the correct oil are the best prevention. Using Mercedes‑approved oil and changing it every 10,000 miles significantly reduces the risk.

What is the difference between the M112 and M272 engines?

The M112 is the older 2.6L, 3.2L, and 3.7L V6 used from 1998–2006. The M272 is the later 3.0L, 3.5L V6 used from 2004–2015. Both have timing system issues, but the failure modes differ.
