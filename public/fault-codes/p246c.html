P246C Mercedes — Diesel Particulate Filter Regeneration Incomplete

P246C is a common DPF fault code on Mercedes diesel models. It means the ECU attempted to perform a regeneration cycle but was unable to complete it – but that can be caused by a low fuel level, a faulty temperature sensor, a blocked DPF, or simply interrupted driving. This page breaks down the causes ranked by cost, what to check first, and whether you should keep driving.

P246C means the ECU attempted a DPF regeneration but couldn't complete it – typically because the vehicle was turned off mid-cycle, fuel was too low, a sensor was faulty, or there's an underlying engine fault. On most Mercedes diesels, this is a straightforward fix, often requiring only a forced regeneration or a sensor replacement.

  • Most common real cause: interrupted regeneration (turning off the engine mid-cycle) – typically £100–£200 for a diagnostic scan and forced regeneration [PATTERN DATA]

What P246C Actually Means

In a Mercedes diesel equipped with a DPF (Diesel Particulate Filter), the ECU periodically initiates a regeneration cycle – a process that raises the temperature inside the DPF to around 600°C to burn off accumulated soot. P246C is logged when the ECU attempts to start or complete a regeneration cycle but is unable to finish it. This could be because the driver turned the engine off mid-cycle, the fuel level was too low, a temperature sensor is faulty and preventing the ECU from starting the regeneration, or the DPF is too blocked to regenerate successfully. The code is a diagnostic flag, not a death sentence – it tells you that a regeneration attempt failed, but it doesn't tell you why. This code appears across the full range of Mercedes common-rail diesels, from the OM651 four‑cylinder to the OM642 V6 and the newer OM654, and the diagnostic approach is similar regardless of which engine triggers it.

Symptoms You'll Notice Alongside It

  • DPF warning light illuminated – often amber
  • Engine management light illuminated, often amber
  • Noticeable loss of power or limp mode, especially under acceleration
  • Increased fuel consumption – the engine may be trying to regenerate
  • Occasional rough idle or hesitation when the DPF is attempting to regenerate
  • A noticeable smell of hot exhaust or burning when regeneration is attempted
  • In some cases, a "DPF Regeneration" message or warning on the dash

Diagnostic Order — Cheapest First

1

Check fuel level and driving history

Ensure fuel level is above 1/4 tank. If the regeneration was interrupted by turning off the engine, a simple forced regeneration may be all that's needed.

2

Read live data: regeneration history and conditions

A specialist can check if the ECU has logged regeneration attempts and see what conditions prevented completion – often the quickest diagnostic.

3

Check temperature sensors for faults

If temperature sensors (P244A/B) are faulty, the ECU won't start regeneration. These are cheap and easy to replace.

4

Inspect for underlying engine faults

Boost leaks, faulty injectors, or EGR issues can prevent reaching regeneration temperature.

5

Only then consider DPF cleaning/replacement

If steps 1–4 are clear and regeneration still fails, the DPF may be too blocked to regenerate.

Can You Still Drive It?

Conditionally, yes – but get it diagnosed within days. P246C indicates that a regeneration attempt has failed. If the DPF warning light is illuminated, you should continue driving as normal to allow the next regeneration attempt to start. However, if the warning light is flashing or the vehicle is in limp mode, prolonged driving is not recommended. Short journeys to a specialist are acceptable, but repeated failed regeneration attempts can lead to a blocked DPF and more expensive repairs.

Related Fault Codes

P246C rarely appears alone. Which codes accompany it materially changes the diagnosis.

P2463 — DPF Soot Accumulation

If you have both, the DPF is likely too blocked to regenerate – cleaning or replacement may be needed.

P244A — DPF Temperature Sensor Circuit

A faulty temperature sensor can prevent regeneration from starting at all.

P244B — DPF Temperature Sensor High Voltage

Similar to P244A – a faulty sensor can prevent the ECU from initiating regeneration.

P2002 — DPF Efficiency Below Threshold

Repeated regeneration failures can cause the DPF to lose efficiency.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drive with P246C on a Mercedes diesel?

Yes – if the DPF warning light is amber, continue driving as normal. The ECU will attempt another regeneration when conditions are right. However, if the light is flashing or the car is in limp mode, avoid long journeys and get it checked promptly.

Does P246C always mean the DPF needs replacing?

No – interrupted regeneration is the most common cause. A simple forced regeneration or topping up the fuel often resolves it. Replacement is only needed if the DPF is too blocked to regenerate.

Will clearing the code fix it?

Only temporarily. The ECU will attempt another regeneration, and if it fails again, the code will return. It's better to address the underlying cause.

How much does a forced DPF regeneration cost?

Expect £100–£200 at an independent Mercedes specialist – often the cheapest fix. Many specialists will include a regeneration as part of a diagnostic check.

What is the 70% rule for scrap vs repair?

For Mercedes, the repair‑to‑value threshold is 70% – if the cost of the repair (including diagnostics and parts) exceeds 70% of the car's current market value, scrapping may be the more sensible financial decision. Always get a current valuation before committing to major work.
