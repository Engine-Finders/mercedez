Mercedes M157 White Smoke From Exhaust

White smoke on this specific AMG V8 is a different conversation to the same symptom on any other Mercedes engine. Here's exactly what it means on the M157, the components most likely to be responsible, and real cost data if it turns out to be the fault this engine is known for.

On the M157 specifically, white smoke from the exhaust is a clear sign of coolant entering the combustion chambers - most commonly caused by a leaking head gasket, a cracked cylinder head, or a failed coolant crossover pipe that is allowing coolant to be drawn into the intake. It's a serious issue that, if ignored, can lead to overheating, loss of lubrication, and catastrophic engine failure.

What This Engine Specifically Does

Where our general white smoke guide covers several possible causes across the whole Mercedes range, on the M157 the picture narrows considerably. This AMG V8's dominant failure signature is a thick, white cloud of steam from the exhaust, often most noticeable on cold start or under hard acceleration, accompanied by a sweet smell of coolant. It is distinct from the black smoke of over-fuelling and the blue smoke of oil burning. On the M157, white smoke almost always points to coolant entering the combustion chambers - most commonly through a leaking head gasket, a cracked cylinder head, or a failed coolant crossover pipe that allows coolant to be drawn into the intake manifold. The presence of white smoke also often coincides with a low coolant level and a persistent sweet smell in the cabin. Ignoring it can quickly lead to overheating and catastrophic engine damage.

Why This Engine In Particular

The M157's hot-vee design places the turbochargers in the valley of the V8, where they generate immense heat. The cooling system, including the plastic coolant crossover pipes, is subjected to extreme thermal cycles, which can cause the pipes to become brittle and develop micro-cracks. These cracks can allow coolant to leak internally, often being drawn into the intake manifold through the cylinder head gasket or through a cracked cylinder head. Additionally, the cylinder heads themselves are prone to cracking, especially around the exhaust valve seats, due to the high operating temperatures. A leaking head gasket can allow coolant to enter the combustion chamber, producing white smoke. This design characteristic is shared with other Mercedes V8s, but the M157's high-performance nature and heat-soak cycles accelerate wear. The same symptom on, say, a M276 V6 would point to a very similar mechanism but with different failure modes.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming the worst, rule out simpler possibilities. Check the coolant level - if it's low, top it up and monitor for recurrence. Check the oil dipstick and oil filler cap for a milky residue, which would indicate coolant in the oil, a clear sign of a head gasket or cracked head issue. A specialist can perform a combustion leak test (block test) to detect exhaust gases in the coolant, confirming a head gasket leak. A pressure test of the cooling system can help locate the source of the leak. If the white smoke is only present on cold start and disappears once warm, it may be normal condensation; if it persists, it's likely coolant. Do not ignore white smoke; it will only get worse and can lead to overheating and catastrophic engine damage.

Verdict

Get it diagnosed by a specialist as soon as you notice white smoke. The M157's white smoke is a clear sign of coolant entering the combustion chamber, and early intervention can save you from a much larger bill. If it's a simple coolant crossover pipe leak, the repair is significant but manageable. If it's a head gasket or cracked head, the cost is higher but still viable compared to a full engine replacement. Frequently Asked Questions

Is white smoke from an M157 always a head gasket issue?

It's a common cause, but not the only one. A cracked cylinder head, a failed coolant crossover pipe, or even a leaking turbocharger coolant line can also cause white smoke. A specialist can diagnose the exact cause with a combustion leak test, a pressure test, and a physical inspection.

At what mileage does an M157 typically start producing white smoke?

Most cases in our data occur between 60,000 and 100,000 miles, though this is heavily dependent on service history and driving style. Regular cooling system inspections are the best prevention.

Can I drive an M157 with white smoke?

It's not recommended. White smoke indicates coolant is entering the combustion chamber, which can lead to overheating, hydro-lock, and catastrophic engine damage. Have it inspected as soon as possible.

How much does it cost to fix white smoke on an M157?

A coolant crossover pipe replacement typically costs £800–£1,500 at an independent specialist. A head gasket replacement can cost £3,000–£6,000. If the engine has suffered severe damage, you're looking at £6,000–£10,000 for a reconditioned engine supply and fit.
