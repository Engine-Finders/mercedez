Mercedes M274 Engine Knocking Noise

A knocking noise from the M274 2.0-litre turbo petrol is a serious warning. This page covers what it specifically means on this engine, the most likely culprits, and real cost data to help you decide what to do next.

On the M274, an engine knocking noise is rarely benign. It most often signals a failing timing chain tensioner, a worn camshaft adjuster, or – in more advanced cases – a bottom-end bearing failure. The noise may be intermittent at cold start or continuous under load. Ignoring it risks catastrophic engine damage. A specialist inspection is essential. Typical repair costs range from £800 for a tensioner to over £2,500 for a full chain and bearing replacement.

What This Engine Specifically Does

On the M274, an engine knocking noise is not a single sound but a family of sounds that vary with engine speed, load, and temperature. At cold start, a brief rattle that disappears after 1–2 seconds is almost certainly the timing chain tensioner (the familiar M274 cold start rattle). However, a continuous metallic knock that gets louder under acceleration or persists when the engine is warm points to more serious issues: a worn camshaft adjuster (VVT phaser) that has lost its locking pin, or – in the worst case – a connecting rod or main bearing failure due to oil starvation or fatigue.

Owners often describe the sound as “a heavy tapping”, “a hammering from the bottom end”, or “a diesel-like clatter on a petrol engine”. Unlike the brief rattle of a failing tensioner, a continuous knock indicates mechanical play that worsens with every revolution. On the M274, which has a high compression ratio and direct injection, a knocking noise is often the first audible warning of a chain jumping a tooth or a bearing beginning to spin – both of which can lead to a complete engine write‑off within a few hundred miles.

Why This Engine In Particular

The M274 is a 2.0‑litre turbocharged four‑cylinder with a rear‑mounted timing chain. This design places the chain and tensioner at the bulkhead end, making them more labour‑intensive to access – but more importantly, it means that any slack in the chain can cause the camshafts to lose alignment, leading to a knocking noise as the valves contact the pistons or the chain slaps against the guides. The tensioner is hydraulically operated and relies on oil pressure; when its internal seals wear or the check valve fails, oil drains back overnight, causing the chain to slap on the next cold start.

Additionally, the camshaft adjuster (phaser) on the intake side is known to wear, causing a rattle that can persist beyond start‑up. If the adjuster’s locking pin fails, the camshaft will oscillate, creating a continuous knocking sound. This is a design characteristic of the M274 and its close relatives (M270); the same symptom on, say, a diesel OM651 would more likely point to injector seals or a different timing issue. The M274’s direct‑injection system also produces a characteristic high‑pressure fuel pump tick, which can be confused with a knock – but a true knock is deeper and more metallic.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming the worst, differentiate between normal direct‑injection noise (a high‑pitched ticking) and a true knock. A knock is a lower, heavier sound that increases with engine load. Use a mechanic’s stethoscope or a long screwdriver to isolate the noise: place it on the cylinder block, the timing cover, and the cam cover. If the noise is loudest at the timing cover and occurs only on cold start, it’s likely the tensioner. If it’s loudest at the bottom of the block and persists when hot, suspect a bearing.

A specialist will perform a diagnostic scan to check for camshaft position deviation (fault codes P0011 or P0016), and may carry out an oil pressure test to rule out bearing wear. Do not ignore a continuous knock – driving even a short distance can turn a £1,000 repair into a £5,000 engine replacement. If you hear a knock, stop driving and have the car recovered to a workshop.

Verdict

Do not ignore a knocking noise on the M274. A brief rattle on cold start is a warning; a continuous knock is a crisis. If caught early (tensioner or phaser), the repair is straightforward and the engine will last many more miles. If left until the chain jumps or a bearing spins, you are facing a £4,000+ bill. On a typical C‑Class or GLC with a market value of £8,000–£25,000, a £1,000–£1,500 repair is a sensible investment. Replacement flags ✅ Safe against our 70% threshold for the majority of M274‑fitted vehicles, but on older lower‑value W204/GLK examples it may be a ⚠️ Watch – full reasoning is explained in our Decision Framework.

The key is to act immediately. Have a specialist inspect the tensioner, camshaft adjusters and oil pressure as soon as you hear any unusual noise. If your car is a high‑mileage W204 or GLK with a current value under £4,000, weigh the repair cost against the vehicle’s worth – but remember that a well‑maintained example may still justify the investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is engine knocking on the M274 always a timing chain issue?

Not always – but it's the most common cause for a cold‑start rattle. A continuous knock under load is more likely to be a camshaft adjuster, a bottom‑end bearing, or even a failed hydraulic lifter. A specialist can differentiate with a stethoscope and diagnostic software.

How much does it cost to fix an M274 engine knock?

At an independent specialist, a tensioner replacement is around £800–£1,200. A full chain and guide set can be £1,500–£2,500. If the bottom end is damaged, expect £3,000–£5,000 for a rebuild. Main dealer prices are higher.

Can I drive my M274 with a knocking noise?

No. Even a cold‑start rattle indicates a tensioner that is losing oil pressure – continued driving can cause the chain to jump. A continuous knock means mechanical wear that will worsen rapidly. Stop driving and have the car recovered to a specialist.

At what mileage does the M274 timing chain tensioner fail?

Most cases occur between 60,000 and 120,000 miles, with a cluster around 80,000–100,000. Regular oil changes with MB‑approved 229.5 oil and avoiding long oil service intervals can significantly reduce the risk.

Is the M274 known for bottom‑end bearing failure?

Less common than timing chain issues, but it does happen – usually as a result of oil starvation, sludge, or prolonged neglect. If the oil pressure light comes on or you hear a deep knock, stop the engine immediately.
