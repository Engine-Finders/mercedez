Mercedes M274 Oil In Coolant

Finding oil in the coolant expansion tank on your M274 2.0-litre turbo petrol is a serious red flag. This page covers exactly what causes it on this specific engine, the real cost to fix it, and our verdict on repair vs replace.

On the M274, oil in the coolant is most commonly caused by a failed oil cooler (integrated with the oil filter housing), a cracked cylinder head, or a blown head gasket. It can also be a sign of a faulty transmission oil cooler in the radiator. Ignoring it can lead to sludge formation, blocked coolant passages, and eventual engine failure. Typical repair costs range from £600–£1,200 for an oil cooler replacement to over £2,500 for a head gasket or cylinder head repair.

What This Engine Specifically Does

On the M274, oil in the coolant is a distinctive symptom that usually presents as a brown, milky, or mayonnaise-like substance floating in the coolant expansion tank. You may also notice a film of oil on the surface of the coolant, a sweet smell mixed with a burnt oil smell, or the coolant level dropping without a visible external leak. Unlike coolant loss from a weeping water pump, which leaves no trace in the oil, oil in coolant indicates a cross-contamination between the two fluid circuits.

This is a serious symptom that should never be ignored. On the M274, the most common source of contamination is a failed oil cooler, which is integrated into the oil filter housing. The oil cooler contains a matrix of oil and coolant passages separated by a gasket or a thin metal plate; when this fails, oil and coolant mix. Less commonly, it can be a cracked cylinder head, a blown head gasket, or, in automatic transmission models, a failed transmission oil cooler inside the radiator.

Why This Engine In Particular

The M274's oil cooler is a compact unit mounted directly on the engine block, adjacent to the oil filter housing. It is a common failure point on this engine family, with the internal gaskets degrading over time due to heat cycling and oil contamination. The design uses a stack of plates for heat exchange, and the seals between these plates can fail, allowing oil at high pressure (up to 5 bar) to leak into the coolant circuit, which operates at a lower pressure (around 1.4 bar).

This is a design characteristic of the M274 and its close relatives (M270). The same symptom on, say, an OM651 diesel would often point to a different failure – typically a cracked oil cooler housing or a failed EGR cooler. On the M274, the oil cooler is the primary suspect, and it's a known issue that becomes more prevalent on higher-mileage vehicles.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

Before assuming the worst, check the coolant expansion tank for any oil film or mayonnaise-like sludge. If present, the next step is to rule out the transmission oil cooler (if your car has an automatic transmission). A specialist can perform a pressure test on both the engine cooling system and the transmission cooler circuit to isolate the source. They may also use a chemical test to detect combustion gases in the coolant, which would point to a head gasket failure.

Do not drive the car with oil in the coolant. The oil can clog the radiator, heater core, and coolant passages, and the coolant can contaminate the oil, reducing its lubricating properties. Have the car recovered to a specialist for a thorough diagnosis.

Verdict

Do not ignore oil in the coolant on the M274. The most likely cause – a failed oil cooler – is common and repairable, and catching it early can save you from a much larger bill. On a typical C‑Class or GLC with a market value of £8,000–£25,000, a £600–£1,200 repair is a sensible investment. Replacement flags ✅ Safe against our 70% threshold for the majority of M274‑fitted vehicles, but on older lower-value W204/GLK examples it may be a ⚠️ Watch – full reasoning is explained in our Decision Framework.

If it's just the oil cooler, the repair is straightforward and the engine will last many more miles. If you let it continue, the contamination can cause sludge formation, block the radiator, and damage the oil pump and bearings. Our advice: have a specialist inspect the oil cooler, head gasket, and transmission cooler as soon as you notice oil in the coolant.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is oil in coolant on the M274 always an oil cooler issue?

It's the most common cause, but not the only one. A cracked cylinder head, a blown head gasket, or a failed transmission oil cooler can also cause oil to mix with coolant. A specialist can diagnose the exact cause with pressure tests and fluid analysis.

How much does it cost to fix oil in coolant on an M274?

At an independent specialist, an oil cooler replacement is around £600–£1,200. A head gasket replacement is £1,000–£2,500. If the cylinder head is cracked, you're looking at £2,500–£4,500. Main dealer prices are higher.

Can I drive my M274 with oil in the coolant?

No. The oil can clog the radiator and heater core, and the coolant can contaminate the oil, reducing its lubricating properties. Have the car recovered to a workshop immediately.

At what mileage does the M274 oil cooler typically fail?

Most cases in our data occur between 60,000 and 120,000 miles. Regular oil and coolant changes with the correct MB‑spec fluids can extend the life of the oil cooler seals.

Is the M274 known for head gasket failure?

Less common than oil cooler failure, but it can happen, especially if the engine has been overheated. If you see white smoke from the exhaust, a milky residue on the oil cap, or a constant low coolant warning, have a specialist check for head gasket failure.
