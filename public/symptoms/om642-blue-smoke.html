Mercedes OM642 Blue Smoke

Blue smoke from the exhaust on the OM642 3.0-litre V6 diesel is a clear sign of oil burning. This page covers exactly what causes it on this engine, the real cost to fix it, and our verdict on repair vs replace.

On the OM642, blue smoke from the exhaust indicates that engine oil is entering the combustion chamber – most commonly due to a leaking turbocharger oil seal, worn valve stem seals, or a faulty PCV valve. This is a known issue on higher‑mileage OM642 engines and can lead to increased oil consumption, fouled spark plugs (glow plugs), and DPF damage. Typical repair costs range from £800–£2,000 for a turbocharger replacement to £1,500–£3,000 for valve stem seal replacement.

What This Engine Specifically Does

On the OM642, blue smoke is a distinct, bluish‑grey cloud that appears most prominently on cold start, after idling for a period, or during heavy acceleration. Unlike the black smoke of an over‑rich mixture or the white smoke of coolant ingress, blue smoke indicates that engine oil is being burned in the combustion chamber. On this V6 diesel, the most common source is a leaking turbocharger oil seal – the seal that prevents oil from entering the compressor side of the turbo. Over time, these seals can wear or harden, allowing oil to be drawn into the intake and burned, producing blue smoke.

Less common but more serious causes include worn valve stem seals (which allow oil to seep down the valve guides) or a failed PCV (positive crankcase ventilation) system, which can draw oil vapour into the intake manifold. The OM642, particularly higher‑mileage examples (over 100k), has a known tendency for turbo seal wear, and this symptom is often the first warning. If left unchecked, the oil consumption can increase, leading to fouled DPF and catalytic converter damage, and eventually engine failure.

Why This Engine In Particular

The OM642 is a 3.0‑litre V6 diesel that uses a turbocharger with variable geometry. The turbocharger is oil‑cooled and relies on seals to keep oil within the bearing housing. Over time, heat cycling and oil degradation can cause these seals to harden and lose their elasticity, allowing oil to leak past the turbine or compressor seals. When this happens, oil enters the intake tract and burns in the cylinders, producing blue smoke. This is a well‑documented issue on the OM642, particularly on vehicles used for short trips where the turbo doesn't get up to temperature regularly, leading to carbon buildup and seal wear.

The same symptom on a petrol M276, for example, would often point to valve stem seals or piston rings, but on the OM642 diesel, the turbocharger oil seal is the primary suspect. The PCV system can also fail, but that is less common. This engine also has a history of swirl flap and oil cooler issues, but those present with different symptoms (black smoke, oil in coolant). Blue smoke on the OM642 is almost always oil‑related and often traced to the turbocharger.

How to Confirm It's This, Not Something Else

The first step is to distinguish blue smoke from other types. Blue smoke has a distinct oily smell and is persistent – it doesn't clear quickly like condensation. Check the oil level regularly; if you're topping up more than 1 litre between services, that's a strong indicator of oil consumption. A specialist can perform a compression test and a leak‑down test to rule out piston ring wear. They may also inspect the turbocharger for oil leaks – removing the intake pipe and checking for oil residue is a common test.

If the blue smoke occurs primarily on cold start and then clears, it could be valve stem seals. If it's continuous under all conditions, it's more likely to be the turbocharger oil seal. Do not ignore blue smoke – it will worsen over time and can lead to DPF failure, which is an expensive repair. Have it inspected as soon as possible.

Verdict

Do not ignore blue smoke on the OM642. The most likely cause – a leaking turbocharger oil seal – is a known and repairable issue. On a typical E‑Class or GLE with a market value of £8,000–£22,000, a £800–£2,000 repair is a sensible investment. Replacement flags ✅ Safe against our 70% threshold for the majority of OM642‑fitted vehicles, but on older lower‑value W211/ML examples it may be a ⚠️ Watch – full reasoning is explained in our Decision Framework.

If you catch it early, the repair is straightforward and the engine will last many more miles. If you let it continue, the oil consumption will increase, and the DPF may become blocked. Our advice: have a specialist perform an oil consumption test and inspect the turbocharger and PCV system as soon as you notice blue smoke.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is blue smoke on the OM642 always a turbocharger oil seal?

It's the most common cause, but not the only one. Worn valve stem seals, a failed PCV valve, or even worn piston rings can cause blue smoke. A specialist can diagnose the exact cause with compression tests and inspection of the turbocharger.

How much does it cost to fix blue smoke on an OM642?

At an independent specialist, a turbocharger replacement is around £800–£2,000. Valve stem seal replacement is £1,500–£3,000. Main dealer prices are higher.

Can I drive my OM642 with blue smoke?

It's not recommended. Blue smoke indicates oil burning, which can foul the DPF, damage the catalytic converter, and increase oil consumption. Have it inspected as soon as possible.

At what mileage does the OM642 turbo oil seal typically fail?

Most cases in our data occur between 80,000 and 150,000 miles. Regular oil changes with the correct MB‑spec oil and avoiding long oil change intervals can slow the degradation.

Can using the wrong oil cause blue smoke on the OM642?

Yes. Using oil that doesn't meet MB 229.5 or 229.51 specification can increase wear on turbo seals and valve stem seals, accelerating oil consumption. Always use the correct oil.
