Airmatic System Explained

Mercedes‑Benz Airmatic is a fully adaptive air suspension system fitted to E‑Class, S‑Class, GLE, GLS, and other premium models. This guide explains how it works, what its key components are, common failure patterns, and what it costs to put right.

Airmatic is an electronically controlled air suspension system that replaces conventional steel springs with air springs (air struts) and an onboard compressor. It offers variable ride height and adaptive damping, but it is also one of the more expensive systems to repair when it fails.

  • Key components: air struts (front and rear), compressor, valve block, air lines, and ride height sensors
  • Common failures: compressor burnout, air strut air leaks (rubber perishes), valve block failure, and moisture ingress in the air supply system
  • Typical repair costs (UK indie): compressor replacement £500–£1,200; single air strut £600–£1,500; full system overhaul £2,500–£5,000+

What Is Airmatic?

Airmatic is Mercedes‑Benz's proprietary air suspension system, first introduced in the early 2000s on the W220 S‑Class and subsequently fitted to E‑Class (W211 onward), CLS, GLE, GLS, and other high‑end models. It uses air‑filled rubber bellows (air struts) at each corner, pressurised by an electric compressor, to support the vehicle's weight. The system is fully adaptive — it adjusts ride height and damping in real time based on driving conditions, speed, and driver input (via the suspension mode selector). Unlike traditional steel springs, Airmatic can lower the car at speed for better aerodynamics, raise it for off‑road clearance on SUVs, and level the vehicle automatically when loaded.

The system is managed by an electronic control unit that receives input from ride height sensors at each wheel, accelerometers, and the vehicle's CAN bus network. When the car is parked, the system may self‑level or enter a "sleep" mode to preserve the battery. Over time, the rubber components degrade, the compressor wears out, and moisture can enter the air system — leading to a range of failures that are among the most common and costly repairs on premium Mercedes models. Common Airmatic Failures & Symptoms

Based on our enquiry data and workshop interviews, the most frequent Airmatic failures are as follows. These are not recalls — they are wear‑and‑tear patterns that become more common as the vehicle ages. [EM-INTERVIEW]

  • Compressor failure — often caused by moisture ingress, which damages the piston and piston rings. Symptoms include the compressor running excessively, the car not rising, or a "car on bump stops" warning in the instrument cluster. Compressors typically fail between 50k and 80k miles, particularly on models where the air dryer is not serviced.
  • Air strut air leaks — the rubber bellows perishes over time, allowing air to escape. A car that sags on one corner after being parked overnight is a classic sign. Leaks can be slow (dropping over several days) or fast (dropping within hours). The strut itself may also suffer from damper wear, causing a bouncy ride even when the air spring is intact.
  • Valve block failure — internal solenoids stick or leak, causing uneven ride height or air loss across all corners. The valve block is located near the compressor and is susceptible to corrosion if moisture is present in the air supply.
  • Moisture / desiccant saturation — the dryer (desiccant) in the air supply system becomes saturated, allowing moisture to pass through to the valve block and struts. This causes corrosion and premature failure of the valve block and lines. Replacing the desiccant periodically is often overlooked but is a relatively low‑cost preventative measure.

For a full list of Airmatic‑related failures, including system warnings and diagnostic codes, see our Failures Hub and specific pages like Airmatic Compressor Failure and Air Suspension Valve Block Failure.

How a Specialist Diagnoses Airmatic Problems

Diagnosing Airmatic faults requires specialist diagnostic equipment and a systematic approach. Here is how a Mercedes specialist will typically proceed:

  1. Read fault codes — using a Mercedes‑specific diagnostic tool (Star Diagnosis), they will scan the Airmatic control unit for stored fault codes. Common codes include C1512 (compressor), C1524 (valve block), and various ride height sensor codes.
  2. Visual inspection — inspect each air strut for signs of air leaks (cracks in the rubber bellows), physical damage, or oil leaks from the damper. Check the compressor and valve block for corrosion or moisture.
  3. Pressure test — using a nitrogen or air pressure test, the system can be pressurised and monitored for leaks. This is often done with a soapy water spray to identify bubbling at joints or on the rubber bellows.
  4. Ride height sensor check — the linkage arms of the ride height sensors are checked for wear or breakage, and the sensor output is verified against actual ride height.
  5. Compressor operation test — the compressor is run to check its output pressure and duty cycle. A weak compressor may still run but fail to achieve the required pressure within the specified time.

If the car is still driveable but the suspension is compromised, a specialist will be able to give you a definitive diagnosis — unlike a generalist garage, they will know the specific failure patterns for your model. You can find a vetted specialist via our registration lookup tool. [EM-VERIFIED]

Verdict: Repair or Replace?

The Airmatic system is expensive to repair, but it is almost always worth fixing unless the cost of repair exceeds approximately 70% of the vehicle's current market value — the Mercedes scrap threshold. [EM-VERIFIED] For example, if your 2010 E‑Class is worth £4,000 and you have a compressor failure costing £1,200 (well under 70%), the repair is easily justified. If the same car needs four new struts and a compressor at a total of £4,500, the repair cost exceeds the vehicle's value — in that case, consider scrapping or selling the car for parts and replacing it with a car that has a working system or coil‑spring conversion (not available on all models). Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my Airmatic compressor is failing?

Common signs include: the compressor running constantly, a longer‑than‑usual time for the car to reach ride height, a "car on bump stops" warning, or a rough, noisy compressor operation. If you hear a loud grinding noise from the front of the car, the compressor bearings may have failed.

How long do Airmatic struts typically last?

In our data, air struts typically last between 60,000 and 100,000 miles, depending on road quality and driving style. Cold climates and salt‑treated roads can accelerate rubber degradation, while heavy loads and frequent off‑road use can stress the system more than motorway cruising. [EM-INTERVIEW]

Can I drive with a failed Airmatic strut?

You can, but you should not drive far or fast. A collapsed corner will cause uneven tyre wear, poor handling, and potentially damage the other struts. If the car is on the bump stops, it is unsafe to drive at all — call for recovery. [EM-INTERVIEW]

Is Airmatic failure covered by warranty?

If the vehicle is still under the original manufacturer warranty, yes — Airmatic is covered like any other mechanical component. On extended warranty policies, check the small print: some exclude air suspension parts, while others cover it with an excess. It is worth checking before authorising a repair. [THIRD-PARTY]

Can I replace just one air strut, or do I need both?

It is strongly recommended to replace struts in pairs (front pair or rear pair) to maintain even ride height and damping characteristics. Replacing a single strut with a new unit while the other side is worn can lead to uneven handling and a shorter lifespan for the new part. [EM-INTERVIEW]
