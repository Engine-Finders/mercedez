Mercedes coolant must meet MB‑Approval 325.0, 325.2 or 325.3 — usually a G48 (ethylene glycol‑based) or G05 (hybrid OAT) formulation, depending on model year. The correct mixing ratio is 50/50 coolant to distilled water, which provides freeze protection down to approximately −37°C.

  • Change interval: 5 years or 100,000 miles, whichever comes first
  • System capacity: Varies by model — typically 8–15 litres total system capacity
  • Never mix different coolant types — G48 and G05 are not compatible and can form sludge

Notes & Caveats

Several factors can affect coolant specification and service life:

  • Never top up with tap water — minerals and chlorides can cause corrosion and scaling. Always use distilled or deionised water when mixing coolant.
  • Mixing G48 and G05 is not recommended — the different inhibitor chemistries can react, forming sludge that blocks radiator passages and heater cores. Flush the system thoroughly if switching types.
  • Some models use different coolants for the engine and the intercooler/charge air system — check your vehicle's specific requirements. [EM-INTERVIEW]
  • Coolant concentration should be checked with a refractometer — not a hydrometer, as glycol content affects the reading. Aim for 50/50, but adjust for extreme climates (e.g., colder regions may run 55/60% coolant).
  • Older Mercedes models (pre‑2005) require G48‑based coolant with a 2‑year change interval — the inhibitor package degrades faster than modern G05 formulations.

Common Mistakes

  • Using universal "all makes" coolant. These often do not meet MB‑Approval standards and can cause corrosion — especially on modern aluminium engines.
  • Topping up with neat coolant. This raises the freezing point and reduces heat transfer — always pre‑mix to the correct ratio.
  • Ignoring the change interval. Old coolant loses its corrosion inhibitors and becomes acidic, attacking the water pump, radiator, and heater matrix.
  • Assuming the same coolant applies to all models. AMG models and diesel engines may have different coolant specifications — check your owner's manual or use our Engine Code Guide to confirm.
  • Not flushing the system when changing coolant type. Residual old coolant can react with the new chemistry — always flush with distilled water before refilling. [EM-INTERVIEW]

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The correct coolant type for your Mercedes is usually stamped on the coolant expansion tank cap, or listed in the owner's manual. You can also check the MB‑Approval number on the coolant bottle — genuine Mercedes coolant will display an approval number (e.g., 325.0, 325.3). To confirm your exact build specification, use our VIN Decoder tool — it will pull your vehicle's original coolant specification from the build data. For pre‑2005 models, a refractometer reading of 50% coolant concentration should indicate freeze protection around −37°C — if it reads lower, adjust concentration accordingly. [EM-INTERVIEW]

Why This Specification Matters

The correct coolant specification is not a minor detail — it is essential to the longevity of the cooling system and the engine itself. Using the wrong coolant can cause:

  • Corrosion — particularly in aluminium engine blocks and radiators, leading to pitting and eventual leakage.
  • Blockage — from sludge formation when incompatible coolants are mixed, reducing cooling efficiency and potentially causing overheating.
  • Water pump failure — incorrect coolant can degrade the water pump seal and impeller, leading to premature failure.
  • Overheating — if the coolant concentration is too low (e.g., too much water) or too high (e.g., too much coolant), heat transfer is reduced and the engine can overheat.

Mercedes cooling systems are designed for the specific chemistry of MB‑Approved coolants — deviations from the specification are a common cause of cooling system failures, which are expensive to repair. The cost of a coolant flush and refill with the correct fluid is negligible compared to the cost of a radiator, water pump, or head gasket replacement.
