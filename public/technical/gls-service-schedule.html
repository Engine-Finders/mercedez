Variant Differences: X166 and X167

The GLS has been produced across two generations: the first‑generation X166 (2015–2019) and the second‑generation X167 (2019–present). Service schedules are broadly similar, but engine options and some fluid specifications differ.

Notes & Caveats

The manufacturer's 15,500‑mile interval is based on European motorway‑weighted driving. In real UK conditions - particularly with diesel OM642 on short urban trips - oil dilution from DPF regeneration can degrade the oil sooner [EM-INTERVIEW]. Many independent Mercedes specialists we've spoken to recommend reducing oil change intervals to 10,000 miles or 12 months for predominantly urban use, regardless of what the service indicator says. This is especially important for X166 diesel models, which share the well‑documented OM642 swirl flap and oil cooler seal vulnerabilities covered on our OM642 Swirl Flap Failure and OM642 Oil Cooler Seal Failure pages.

Brake fluid absorbs moisture over time - the 2‑year interval is not a suggestion. Ignoring it can lead to internal corrosion of the ABS pump and reduced braking performance, especially in emergency stops. Similarly, the coolant change at 10 years uses a long‑life formulation, but if you're topping up with tap water (common mistake), you should change it much sooner.

The 9G‑Tronic transmission fluid is not officially a 'lifetime' fill; many specialists recommend a fluid and filter change at 60,000 miles to extend gearbox life and avoid the expensive valve body issues that can occur on neglected units [EM-INTERVIEW]. We cover this in more detail on our 7G‑Tronic / 9G‑Tronic Faults page.

Common Mistakes

  • Ignoring the brake fluid interval. “It looks fine” is not a valid test; moisture content must be measured. Replace every 2 years regardless.
  • Using the wrong oil specification. Petrol engines require MB‑Approval 229.5; diesels require 229.51 (low‑SAPS) - using the wrong one can damage the DPF and after‑treatment systems. Check your engine code first via our Engine Code Decoder.
  • Not resetting the service indicator. Even if you do the work, the car's computer still counts down based on time and mileage; an overdue warning can affect resale value.
  • Assuming the transmission fluid is 'lifetime'. Mercedes no longer claims lifetime fill for 9G‑Tronic; many specialists recommend a fluid and filter change at 60,000 miles to extend gearbox life [EM-INTERVIEW].
  • Forgetting the AdBlue refill (diesel). The system has its own tank and a warning light; running out can prevent engine restart. Check level during every service.
  • Overlooking the cabin filter. A clogged cabin filter reduces airflow and can affect the air‑conditioning system's performance and smell.

How to Verify on Your Actual GLS

The service history is stored in the car's digital service booklet (accessible via the steering wheel buttons) and can also be retrieved by a Mercedes dealer using the VIN. To confirm which service items are due, use the dashboard service menu: navigate to Service → Service Due. The car will show a countdown in days or miles. For a more detailed check, plug an OBD‑II scanner that reads MB‑specific codes (e.g., via X‑entry or a high‑end generic tool) to read the service counter and any pending maintenance reminders. Always cross‑reference with the physical service book if you have one - discrepancies between digital and paper records are a red flag.

If you're unsure which engine variant you have, locate the engine number stamp (see our Engine Number Location guide) or use the VIN decoder linked below.

Why This Schedule Matters

Following the manufacturer's service schedule is the single most important factor in preserving your GLS's reliability and resale value. A missed oil change can lead to sludge formation, blocked oil pick‑up pipes, and eventually catastrophic engine failure - particularly on the OM642 diesel, where the oil cooler seal and swirl flap issues are sensitive to oil quality and cooling system condition. Brake fluid neglect can cause ABS pump failure, a repair that often exceeds the cost of a full service. Coolant that's past its life loses corrosion inhibition, leading to heater core or radiator blockages that are expensive to flush. By sticking to the schedule - and adjusting for severe use - you avoid these predictable failures. The same logic applies to transmission fluid: a 60,000‑mile change costs a few hundred pounds; a new 9G‑Tronic costs several thousand.

If you're considering buying a used GLS, check the service history meticulously. GLS models with incomplete or deferred services are far more likely to develop the issues detailed on our Failure Pages. Conversely, a fully stamped history with evidence of the major services is a strong indicator of a well‑maintained car.
