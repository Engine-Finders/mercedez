Notes & Caveats

The M178's hot‑vee architecture — with turbochargers positioned inside the V of the engine — creates significantly higher under‑bonnet temperatures than conventional layouts. This heat soak accelerates the degradation of plastic chain guides and can cause the hydraulic tensioner to lose oil pressure on hot restart, leading to chain rattle and eventual stretch [EM-INTERVIEW].

Independent specialists we've spoken to frequently recommend a chain inspection at 60,000 miles, regardless of what the service schedule indicates. If you hear a rattle on cold start that lasts more than 1–2 seconds, this is an early warning of tensioner failure. Addressing it early — replacing the tensioner and guides — costs around £800–£1,500 at a specialist. Ignoring it can lead to chain skip, bent valves, and a full engine rebuild or replacement, which on an M178 can cost £10,000–£18,000 [EM-INTERVIEW].

We cover the full failure mechanism on our M157 Timing Chain Adjuster Failure page (the M178 shares many of the same hot‑vee dynamics). The key takeaway: the chain itself is robust, but the tensioner and guides are the weak points — and they're accelerated by the hot‑vee design.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming the M178 has a belt. It does not — it has a chain. If a seller or mechanic tells you otherwise, they are misinformed.
  • Ignoring cold‑start rattle. A brief rattle on start‑up is not normal; it indicates tensioner wear and should be investigated immediately.
  • Using the wrong oil specification. The hydraulic tensioner relies on oil pressure; using non‑MB‑Approved 229.5 oil can reduce tensioner performance and accelerate wear — see our M178 Oil Capacity page for details.
  • Confusing M178 with M157. The older 5.5‑litre twin‑turbo V8 (M157) also uses a chain but has a different tensioner design and failure pattern — check your engine code first via our Engine Code Decoder.
  • Not inspecting the guides during chain work. Replacing the chain without replacing the guides and tensioner is a false economy — the guides are usually the source of the problem.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The timing chain is not directly visible without removing the engine front cover, but you can check for chain wear indirectly:

  • Listen for cold‑start rattle: A rattle lasting more than 1–2 seconds on a cold start is the most common early indicator of tensioner wear.
  • Check for chain noise at idle: A rhythmic rattling or slapping noise at idle, particularly when hot, may indicate chain stretch or guide wear.
  • Inspect oil condition: If the oil is sludgy or has metallic particles, the chain guides may be wearing.
  • Use diagnostic tools: A Mercedes‑specific diagnostic tool can read camshaft position deviation values — if the deviation exceeds specification, the chain may be stretched.

For definitive confirmation, the front timing cover must be removed for visual inspection — a job that requires specialist knowledge. Use our Engine Number Location guide to confirm your engine variant.

Why This Specification Matters

Knowing whether the M178 uses a timing chain or belt matters because it affects maintenance planning, cost expectations, and failure risk assessment. A chain is generally more durable than a belt — it doesn't require routine replacement at a fixed mileage interval — but it is not maintenance‑free. The M178's chain tensioner and guides are known wear points, accelerated by the hot‑vee design.

If you're considering buying an M178‑powered car — such as an AMG GT, E63, S63, or C63 — check the service history for evidence of chain tensioner or guide replacement. Cars that have had this work done (especially with the revised tensioner) are far less likely to experience the catastrophic failure described on our failure page (similar principles apply to the M178). Conversely, a car with no chain work and a cold‑start rattle should be approached with caution — the repair cost can exceed £3,000 at a specialist, and significantly more at a main dealer.
