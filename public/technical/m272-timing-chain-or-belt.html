The Mercedes‑Benz M272 uses a timing chain, not a belt [PLACEHOLDER — confirm vs OEM workshop data]. The chain drives the camshafts and is located at the front of the engine, with hydraulic tensioners and plastic guides that require periodic inspection. The M272 is notorious for balance shaft and timing chain tensioner issues, making correct maintenance critical.

  • Chain type: Duplex roller chain with hydraulic tensioners [PLACEHOLDER — confirm spec]
  • Recommended inspection interval: every 60,000 miles or 5 years (specialist recommendation) [EM-INTERVIEW]
  • Balance shaft gear wear is a known failure point on early M272 engines [PATTERN DATA] — see below
  • Applies across C‑Class (W203/W204), E‑Class (W211/W212), CLS (C219), SLK (R171), and other models

Notes & Caveats

The M272 is widely known for timing chain and balance shaft issues, particularly on early engines (2004–2007). The balance shaft gear is driven by the timing chain; the gear teeth can wear down due to a metallurgical issue, causing the chain to skip, resulting in catastrophic engine failure [PATTERN DATA]. This is so well‑documented that Mercedes issued a technical service bulletin (TSB) and extended warranty coverage in some markets.

Independent specialists we've spoken to recommend a chain inspection at 60,000 miles, regardless of what the service schedule indicates. If you hear a rattle on cold start that lasts more than 1–2 seconds, this is an early warning of tensioner or chain wear. Addressing it early — replacing the tensioner, guides, and balance shaft if necessary — costs around £800–£1,500 at a specialist. Ignoring it can lead to chain skip, bent valves, and a full engine rebuild or replacement, which on an M272 can cost £4,000–£6,000 [EM-INTERVIEW].

We cover the full failure mechanism on our M272 Balance Shaft Chain Failure page. The key takeaway: the chain itself is robust, but the balance shaft gear and tensioner are the weak points — and they're accelerated by poor oil maintenance.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming the M272 has a belt. It does not — it has a chain. If a seller or mechanic tells you otherwise, they are misinformed.
  • Ignoring cold‑start rattle. A brief rattle on start‑up is not normal; it indicates tensioner or balance shaft wear and should be investigated immediately.
  • Using the wrong oil specification. The hydraulic tensioner relies on oil pressure; using non‑MB‑Approved 229.5 oil can reduce tensioner performance and accelerate wear — see our M272 Oil Capacity page for details.
  • Confusing M272 with M273. The 5.5‑litre V8 has a different chain design and failure pattern — check your engine code first via our Engine Code Decoder.
  • Not inspecting the balance shaft during chain work. Replacing the chain without checking the balance shaft gear is a false economy — if the gear is worn, it will destroy the new chain quickly.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The timing chain is not directly visible without removing the engine front cover, but you can check for chain wear indirectly:

  • Listen for cold‑start rattle: A rattle lasting more than 1–2 seconds on a cold start is the most common early indicator of tensioner or balance shaft wear.
  • Check for chain noise at idle: A rhythmic rattling or slapping noise at idle, particularly when hot, may indicate chain stretch or guide wear.
  • Inspect oil condition: If the oil is sludgy or has metallic particles, the chain guides or balance shaft may be wearing.
  • Use diagnostic tools: A Mercedes‑specific diagnostic tool can read camshaft position deviation values — if the deviation exceeds specification, the chain may be stretched or the balance shaft gear worn.

For definitive confirmation, the front timing cover must be removed for visual inspection — a job that requires specialist knowledge. Use our Engine Number Location guide to confirm your engine variant.

Why This Specification Matters

Knowing whether the M272 uses a timing chain or belt matters because it affects maintenance planning, cost expectations, and failure risk assessment. A chain is generally more durable than a belt — it doesn't require routine replacement at a fixed mileage interval — but it is not maintenance‑free. The M272's chain tensioner, guides, and balance shaft are known wear points, and early engines have a specific design flaw that makes failure almost inevitable without proactive attention.

If you're considering buying an M272‑powered car — such as a C350, E350, CLS350, or SLK350 — check the service history for evidence of chain tensioner, guide, or balance shaft replacement. Cars that have had this work done (especially with the revised balance shaft) are far less likely to experience the catastrophic failure described on our failure page. Conversely, a car with no chain work and a cold‑start rattle should be approached with caution — the repair cost can exceed £2,000 at a specialist, and significantly more at a main dealer.
