Notes & Caveats

The M274 is known for timing chain tensioner issues, which are directly linked to oil quality and change frequency [PATTERN DATA]. The timing chain tensioner relies on oil pressure to maintain proper tension. Sludge or degraded oil can cause the tensioner to fail, leading to chain slack and eventual catastrophic engine failure. This is a particular concern on the M274, which shares design similarities with the M270 but has a revised tensioner design.

Independent specialists we've spoken to recommend a chain inspection at 60,000 miles, regardless of what the service schedule indicates. If you hear a rattle on cold start that lasts more than 1–2 seconds, this is an early warning of tensioner wear. Addressing it early — replacing the tensioner and guides — costs around £500–£1,000 at a specialist. Ignoring it can lead to chain skip, bent valves, and a full engine rebuild or replacement, which on an M274 can cost £3,000–£6,000 [EM-INTERVIEW].

We cover the full failure mechanism on our M274 Timing Chain Tensioner Failure page. The key takeaway: the chain itself is robust, but the tensioner is the weak point — and it's accelerated by poor oil maintenance.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming the M274 has a belt. It does not — it has a chain. If a seller or mechanic tells you otherwise, they are misinformed.
  • Ignoring cold‑start rattle. A brief rattle on start‑up is not normal; it indicates tensioner wear and should be investigated immediately.
  • Using the wrong oil specification. The hydraulic tensioner relies on oil pressure; using non‑MB‑Approved 229.5 oil can reduce tensioner performance and accelerate wear — see our M274 Oil Capacity page for details.
  • Confusing M274 with M270. The earlier 2.0‑litre turbo (M270) has a different tensioner design and failure pattern — check your engine code first via our Engine Code Decoder.
  • Not inspecting the guides during chain work. Replacing the chain without replacing the guides and tensioner is a false economy — the guides are usually the source of the problem.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The timing chain is not directly visible without removing the engine front cover, but you can check for chain wear indirectly:

  • Listen for cold‑start rattle: A rattle lasting more than 1–2 seconds on a cold start is the most common early indicator of tensioner wear.
  • Check for chain noise at idle: A rhythmic rattling or slapping noise at idle, particularly when hot, may indicate chain stretch or guide wear.
  • Inspect oil condition: If the oil is sludgy or has metallic particles, the chain guides may be wearing.
  • Use diagnostic tools: A Mercedes‑specific diagnostic tool can read camshaft position deviation values — if the deviation exceeds specification, the chain may be stretched.

For definitive confirmation, the front timing cover must be removed for visual inspection — a job that requires specialist knowledge. Use our Engine Number Location guide to confirm your engine variant.

Why This Specification Matters

Knowing whether the M274 uses a timing chain or belt matters because it affects maintenance planning, cost expectations, and failure risk assessment. A chain is generally more durable than a belt — it doesn't require routine replacement at a fixed mileage interval — but it is not maintenance‑free. The M274's chain tensioner is a known wear point, and poor oil maintenance accelerates its failure.

If you're considering buying an M274‑powered car — such as a C200, C300, E200, E300, or GLC 250 — check the service history for evidence of chain tensioner or guide replacement. Cars that have had this work done (especially with the revised tensioner on later models) are far less likely to experience catastrophic failure. Conversely, a car with no chain work and a cold‑start rattle should be approached with caution — the repair cost can exceed £1,500 at a specialist, and significantly more at a main dealer.
