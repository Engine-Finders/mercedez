OM642 Timing Chain Or Belt?

The OM642 V6 diesel — fitted across the C-Class (W204/W205), E-Class (W211/W212), ML/GLE (W164/W166), GL (X164), Sprinter and more — uses a timing chain, not a belt. This page documents the specification, how the chain is driven, what wears, and how to verify what you have.

The OM642 is chain-driven. Mercedes specified a timing chain (not a belt) for the 3.0-litre V6 diesel, with a duplex chain arrangement driving both camshafts from a single tensioner. There is no official replacement interval — the chain is designed for the life of the engine — but tensioner wear is a documented failure point, usually becoming audible as a rattle on cold start from around 70,000–120,000 miles. Repair cost to replace the chain and tensioner is typically £1,200–£2,200 at an independent specialist.

The Specification: OM642 Timing Chain Or Belt

The OM642 is a 3.0-litre V6 diesel, first introduced in 2005. It uses a timing chain — specifically a duplex (two-row) chain — to drive the camshafts from the crankshaft. The chain runs around four sprockets: one on the crankshaft, two on the camshafts (one per bank) and a guide wheel with a hydraulic tensioner.

Real-World Caveats

Mercedes never specified a timing chain replacement interval for the OM642 — it was designed as a "life of engine" component. In practice, the hydraulic tensioner is the weakest point. The tensioner relies on oil pressure to maintain chain tension; as it wears, it can leak down when the engine is off, allowing the chain to slacken slightly. On cold start, this produces a brief metallic rattle until oil pressure builds — usually 1–2 seconds. If the rattle persists or gets louder, the tensioner is failing and, if left, the chain can skip a tooth or, in extreme cases, break.

Specialists we've spoken to recommend replacing the tensioner as a preventative measure if the engine is already being worked on (for example, during an oil cooler seal replacement, a known OM642 weakness [EM-INTERVIEW]). The parts cost for the tensioner is modest (typically £100–£200) compared to the labour to access it, which can be significant on V-engine installations. Full chain replacement is a major job — 10–14 hours of labour — and often includes the guides, tensioner and both sprockets if wear is evident. Common Mistakes

  • Assuming OM642 uses a belt. It doesn't. Some Mercedes four-cylinder diesels use belts, but the V6 is chain-driven. Mistaking it for a belt and quoting a belt-service interval is wrong — see our Engine Code Decoder to confirm your engine family.
  • Trusting the "life of engine" claim blindly. Mercedes' design assumption assumes regular oil changes with the correct spec. The tensioner is oil-pressure-dependent; neglected oil changes accelerate tensioner wear substantially. See our OM642 oil capacity and grade page for the correct specification.
  • Ignoring the cold-start rattle. A brief rattle on startup is often dismissed as "normal". It isn't — it's the warning sign of a tensioner that's losing its ability to hold pressure. Addressing it early (£200–£400 for a tensioner replacement) is far cheaper than the full chain job or an engine replacement if the chain skips.
  • Confusing OM642 with OM651. The OM651 is a four-cylinder diesel and has a completely different timing arrangement — it's also chain-driven but with a different architecture. Use our OM642 vs OM651 comparison if you're unsure which you have.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

To confirm you have an OM642, check the engine number stamp — it will have 642 as the first three digits. The engine number location varies by chassis: on E-Class and C-Class it is typically on the front left side of the block, visible from above once the engine cover is removed. On the Sprinter, it is on the right side of the block (driver's side, UK). Full location guidance is available on our engine number location guide.

To verify which timing system is fitted, the timing cover on the OM642 has a distinctive trapezoidal shape with the tensioner access plug visible at the front. The absence of a belt-driven camshaft arrangement is visually obvious once the fan and shroud are removed — if you see a chain and sprocket behind a metal cover, it's chain-driven.
