OM646 Timing Chain Or Belt?

The definitive answer: the OM646 2.2‑litre CDI diesel uses a timing chain, not a belt. This reference covers chain design, inspection intervals, variant differences and the real‑world caveats that matter to owners and specialists.

The OM646 uses a timing chain, not a belt. The chain drives the camshafts directly from the crankshaft via a duplex chain arrangement, and is designed for the life of the engine under normal service conditions.

Chain Design Explained

The OM646 uses a duplex roller chain — two rows of rollers running side‑by‑side — which provides greater load capacity and redundancy compared to a simplex chain. The chain runs from the crankshaft sprocket up to the camshaft sprockets, with a hydraulic tensioner taking up slack via engine oil pressure. Guide rails keep the chain in alignment and dampen vibration.

Unlike a belt, the chain is not subject to a fixed replacement interval in the manufacturer's service schedule. Mercedes‑Benz considers it a lifetime component, provided the engine is serviced with the correct oil and the tensioner receives adequate oil pressure. In practice, specialist consensus is that chain wear becomes a consideration beyond 150,000–180,000 miles, particularly on vehicles that have experienced oil starvation or extended intervals [EM‑INTERVIEW]. The chain itself rarely breaks; the more common failure mode is tensioner wear leading to rattling on cold start, which can then allow the chain to skip teeth on the sprocket — a catastrophic event. Early detection through regular oil analysis and listening for cold‑start rattle is the most effective mitigation.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

  • Assuming it's a belt. The OM646 is chain‑driven — never attempt to fit a belt to this engine. A belt simply does not exist in the parts catalogue.
  • Ignoring cold‑start rattle. A brief rattle on cold start, lasting less than a second, is often dismissed as normal. On higher‑mileage OM646 units, it can indicate a failing tensioner or stretched chain — and ignoring it risks chain jump and valve/piston contact.
  • Believing the chain never needs attention. While there's no scheduled interval, specialist experience shows that chain and tensioner wear is real on high‑mileage or poorly‑serviced engines — see our OM646 injector seal failure page for related oil contamination issues that can accelerate tensioner wear.
  • Using the wrong oil viscosity. The tensioner is hydraulically actuated by engine oil pressure. Using an oil that's too thick (e.g., 10W‑40) on a cold UK morning can delay tensioner take‑up, increasing wear. Always use the specified 5W‑30 low‑SAPS.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The quickest way to confirm whether your engine is chain‑driven is to look up the engine code — all OM646 variants are chain. You can also listen for the characteristic chain rattle on cold start (if present) or inspect the timing cover: the OM646 has a metal timing cover, not the plastic cover typical of belt‑driven engines. Real‑World Caveats & Specialist Insight

While the OM646 chain is robust, independent specialists we've spoken to highlight several real‑world factors that affect chain longevity [EM‑INTERVIEW]:

  • Oil quality is everything. The hydraulic tensioner relies on clean oil at the correct pressure. Extended oil change intervals (beyond 15,000 miles) or using non‑MB‑approved oil can lead to tensioner sludge and premature wear.
  • Short journeys increase risk. Cold starts cause the chain to stretch slightly each time, and the tensioner takes a moment to pressurise. Over thousands of cold starts, this micro‑wear accumulates.
  • High‑mileage inspection. Many specialists recommend a chain and tensioner inspection at around 150,000 miles, even though there's no official interval. This involves checking camshaft timing deviation via diagnostic tools — a deviation of more than 4° crank is a strong indicator of chain stretch.
  • DPF‑related oil contamination. On vehicles used predominantly for short urban trips, DPF regeneration can introduce fuel into the oil, reducing its lubricity and accelerating chain and tensioner wear. This is covered in detail on our DPF & Emissions Systems Explained page.
