Notes & Caveats

The manufacturer's 15,000‑mile interval assumes motorway‑weighted use. In real UK conditions, short urban journeys prevent the DPF from regenerating fully, which can lead to unburned fuel diluting the oil — a well‑documented characteristic of the OM654 family [EM‑INTERVIEW], covered in detail on our OM654 injector seal failure page. Independent specialists we've spoken to frequently recommend a shorter, 6,000‑mile interval for owners whose driving is predominantly short urban trips, regardless of what the service computer indicates. This isn't manufacturer guidance — it's a practical adjustment specialists make based on what they see coming through the workshop, and it costs a fraction of the alternative, which is oil dilution serious enough to require attention.

Common Mistakes

  • Trusting the service computer interval blindly. It doesn't know your driving pattern is mostly short urban trips.
  • Using a non‑low‑SAPS oil. This can damage the DPF and after‑treatment system over time — see our DPF & Emissions Systems Explained for why this matters.
  • Confusing OM654 with OM651 or OM646. The older engines have different capacities and grades — check your engine code first via our Engine Code Guide.
  • Assuming all OM654 variants share identical figures. While capacity is similar, oil cooler layout and filter access can vary; always confirm by checking the engine number stamp.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The dipstick and filler cap on the OM654 are both clearly marked and accessible from above without removing any panels. Confirm your exact build spec via the engine number stamp described on our Engine Number Location guide before assuming these figures apply — early and late production runs occasionally carry minor running changes that aren't always reflected in aftermarket reference sources.

Why This Specification Matters

Getting oil spec wrong on a modern CDI engine isn't a minor issue the way it might have been on an older, simpler engine. The low‑SAPS specification exists specifically to protect the diesel particulate filter and the wider after‑treatment system — using a standard, non‑low‑SAPS oil introduces ash‑forming additives that accumulate in the DPF over time, gradually reducing its capacity to regenerate and eventually requiring an expensive replacement that correct oil choice would have avoided entirely. The viscosity grade matters similarly: 5W‑30 is specified for cold‑weather flow characteristics that directly affect how quickly oil reaches the bearings on a cold UK morning, which connects directly to the failure patterns described on our OM654 injector seal failure page. This is also why we treat "any 5W‑30 will do" as a genuine misconception worth correcting rather than a pedantic technicality — the low‑SAPS approval matters as much as the viscosity number, and cheaper oils meeting the viscosity spec without the emissions‑system approval are a false economy that shows up as a much larger bill years later.
